N-Back Test

How this is scored. Most trials in an n-back run are not targets, so “percent correct” is a trap: press nothing at all and you still score around 72%, having caught nothing. The headline figure is d′ (d-prime) — the distance between how a target feels to you and how a non-target feels, in standard deviations, worked out from your hit rate and your false-alarm rate together. 0 means you cannot tell them apart. Around 1 is a real but shaky signal. Above 3 is a near-flawless session. Answering the same way on every trial scores exactly 0, whichever way it was.

    How the n-back test works

    Each trial shows one lit square for half a second. Your job is to compare it with the square from n trials ago and press Match if they are in the same place. At 2-back you are holding two positions and updating both every two and a half seconds; at 3-back, three. That constant discarding of the oldest item while a new one arrives is what makes n-back a working-memory task rather than a memory-span one — nothing is ever safely stored.

    A session is three blocks of about twenty trials. The first n trials of each block have nothing behind them to match, so they are dealt but never scored.

    The level moves on its own

    Three clean blocks in a row — every target caught, no false alarms — and you go up a rung. Two missed targets in a single block and you drop one. Your rung is remembered between visits, so the task keeps sitting just past what you can comfortably do, which is the only setting at which this kind of practice is worth anything.

    The level itself is deliberately not the number we chart. It is an integer that lives between 1 and 5 for essentially everyone, so a graph of it would be a flat line with the occasional step. It is a badge; the chart plots your d′, which moves continuously and can improve while your level stays put.

    Today's boards are fixed

    Each block is dealt from today's date together with the rung you are on, so reloading the page will not deal you an easier board — the next block is simply the next one. Tomorrow is a fresh set. Note that this does not mean everyone gets the same board: a target is defined relative to n, so a player at 2-back and a player at 4-back cannot meaningfully share a sequence.

    What n-back training does and does not do

    N-back is the most-studied working-memory training task there is, and the honest summary is that people reliably get better at n-back. Whether that improvement carries over to unrelated abilities is contested, and has been for over a decade — treat any site promising IQ points from a browser game with suspicion. What you can expect is a measurable, trackable improvement on this specific task, which is why the chart here plots your own sessions and nothing else.

    Nothing you do here leaves your device: your level, streak and session history live in this browser's local storage, and there is no account and no server.
